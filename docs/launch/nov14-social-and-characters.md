### Character introduction copy

**Nova — the first friendly face**

Nova keeps a warm place at every unfamiliar table. In Lantern Harbor, nobody has to pretend they already know the rules. Nova welcomes you, opens practice and turns a big new game into one small, manageable move. The pink clan’s promise: there is always room to begin again.

**Rocco — the patient tactician**

Rocco notices the reply hiding behind the obvious move. Focused, competitive and kind, the red legion helps you spot threats, compare choices and learn from the turn that surprised you. A good game is a conversation between plans. Rocco teaches you to listen to the other side.

**Piko — keeper of routes and rules**

Piko carries the map when the board gets complicated. From palace boundaries to a piece’s legal path, the blue order makes the next step clear. Piko also opens the game record and explains the evidence available for it—without asking you to understand every technical detail first.

**Ziggy — finder of quiet possibilities**

Ziggy loves the moment when a puzzling circle finally makes sense. The green tribe watches sowing paths, tries another route and finds pleasure in a careful retry. With Ziggy, progress can be a solved puzzle, a useful discovery or simply understanding why the last attempt changed the board.

## Cognitive claims

Prefer descriptions of what a player does: “compare replies,” “follow a route,” “plan around uncertainty,” “notice patterns in this position,” or “try a new puzzle.” These describe gameplay. Do not claim “boosts IQ,” “rewires the brain,” “improves memory,” “prevents disease,” or “better for the brain than chess.” No app-specific controlled evidence was found for those claims. Original active-control chess experiments found no statistically significant mathematical or metacognitive advantage; an EEF school trial also found no attainment benefit. Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28646322/ and https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/chess-in-primary-schools.

Frame “better than chess” as a preference: “If you want a three-player strategy night, try this table.” Do not turn preference into a measured cognitive superiority claim.
